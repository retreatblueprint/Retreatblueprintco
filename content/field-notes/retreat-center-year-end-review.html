Why a year-end review beats a feeling

The numbers show where the money and the effort actually went.

In the retreats I work with, the owners who plan best next season are the ones who can say, in numbers, what this season did. They are also the ones who prepare for high season weeks ahead.

Business success: how the year went

These five show whether the year worked for the business.

NumberWhat it says about your retreatWhat to do with it
New retreat leadersWhether your venue is winning new hosts or relying on the same few. It shows your reach today and how full your pipeline is for next year.Ask each new leader how they found you, and do more of that.
Repeat retreat leadersLeaders who hosted before and booked again, or have dates next year. The surest sign that hosting with you works.Offer next year's dates to them first. If few return, ask the ones who left why.
Repeat guestsWhether guests loved the stay enough to come back. A low count points to the guest experience. A high count means your guest list is already marketing for you.Invite last year's guests to rebook before you advertise.
Profit per retreatWhich retreats earn their place on your calendar once the cost of running them is taken out.Give more dates to the most profitable retreats. Reprice or drop the ones that lose money.
Total profit for the yearWhether your center is financially healthy. A full calendar does not guarantee it.Compare it with last year and set next year's target. Full but low on profit? Look at costs and pricing.

Future-driven: where next year comes from

These four point at next year's bookings.

NumberWhat it says about next yearWhat to do with it
Retreats per nicheWhich kind of retreat (yoga, wellness, corporate, writing) is gaining the most popularity at your venue: where selling is already easy and where you need to work harder.Lean into the niche that is growing. Give a slow one a plan, or fewer dates.
Retreats booked per platformWhich channels actually bring bookings, not only views. It shows where next year's bookings will come from.Put your time and fees where bookings come from. Rethink platforms that bring inquiries but few bookings.
Inquiry-to-booking rateHow well your replies and follow-up turn inquiries into bookings. Low means your inquiry conversations have room to improve. High means they are working.If it is low, reply faster, answer fully and follow up. Find the step where inquiries drop: no reply, a slow reply, no follow-up, or no deposit. Fix that step first.
Reviews and 5-star shareHow many reviews you gained this year and how many were five stars, across Google, booking platforms, TripAdvisor and social media hashtags. It is what your next guest reads before they inquire.Ask every happy guest for a review before they leave. Read the lower ones for a pattern.

Team and guest experience: what holds it together

These two show whether you can deliver the stay next year.

NumberWhat it says about your retreatWhat to do with it
Team retentionHow many of your team stayed through the year. A steady team gives guests the same stay at every retreat. People leaving is an early warning.Ask anyone who left why, before high season. Plan cover for key roles.
Guest experience scoreThe average rating from a short feedback form after each retreat. It shows how guests rate the stay itself, not only whether they come back.Read the comments for a problem that shows up twice, and fix it before high season.

Where to find these numbers

Each one already lives somewhere you use. Bookings, platforms and niches sit in your booking system. Profit sits in your accounting records. Reviews sit on each platform. Team retention sits in your staff list, and guest experience comes from a short form after each retreat.

No system yet? Start with one spreadsheet this year. Add a row for each retreat, with columns for its leader, niche, platform, revenue, cost and guest rating, and fill it in after every retreat. Next year, build your dashboard from it.

A routine for tracking and reviewing your numbers

  1. Every week. Log each inquiry and booking in one place.
  2. Every month. Check your data for 20 minutes and note what moved, so you keep improving as you go.
  3. Q4. Review everything that happened this year. Read the year as a story: what grew, what slipped, what to change. Write it on one page: the numbers, the story, the changes.
  4. Q1. Put the new changes and systems in place.
  5. Q2. Check that everything is working, using your monthly numbers.

A dashboard that pulls these 11 numbers from your booking, accounting and review systems turns the monthly check into a quick look, so Q4 is spent deciding, not collecting. A number you review once a year can only explain the past. A number you review monthly changes the season.
