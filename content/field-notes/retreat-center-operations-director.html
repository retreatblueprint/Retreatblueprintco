The short answer

  1. When does a retreat center need an operations director? When the team waits on you for decisions, the standards live in your head, and no one owns the numbers or the plan for next season.
  2. What does an operations director do? Runs the weekly operating rhythm, writes and enforces the standards, tracks occupancy and profit per retreat, and plans the next season.
  3. Do I need one full time? No. A fractional operations director does the same job for a set number of days a month.

Your team waits on you for decisions

A guest asks for an exception, a supplier needs a yes, a team member wants to know which room to use. Each one is small, and together they fill your day.

{{photo:photo-1768055105012-4c53791b2877|A hand ticking boxes on a handwritten list in a notebook|Count the decisions your team brought you this week.}}

Count how many times your team came to you for a decision this week. An operations director owns those decisions inside agreed limits, so your team gets an answer without waiting for you.

Standards live in your head

New staff learn by following you around. Check-in, room turnover and retreat-day schedules run well when you are there and differently when you are not.

{{photo:photo-1768158989131-64cbff67f292|Shelves of ring binders in green, red, blue and white, each with a labelled spine|Written standards, filed where the whole team can find them.}}

Written standards (SOPs) are the first thing an operations director builds, then trains the team on and checks. Your team stops asking you how it is done, and a guest gets the same experience whoever is on shift.

You cannot see next season without searching

To know how full next quarter is, you check a calendar, a message thread and a spreadsheet, then add it up yourself. A retreat center that runs on three tabs and your memory cannot forecast.

{{photo:photo-1783115259399-3a5a3e0e4592|Hands typing on a laptop that shows a long spreadsheet, with a notebook and pen beside it|One view of the numbers, instead of three tabs and your memory.}}

An operations director keeps one view of occupancy, bookings by retreat and the cash coming in, and reviews it with you every month. The year-end review shows which numbers to start with.

Pricing and staffing are decided by feel

You set rates and rosters from experience and instinct, because the data to check them is not in one place. In the retreats I work with, owners often cannot say what a retreat actually earns after staff, food and facility costs.

An operations director works out profit per retreat and builds staffing around the calendar. Decisions come from the numbers, with your judgment on top.

Growth keeps slipping to next month

A new retreat format, a second location, a better booking engine: the idea is clear and the time is never there. While you work in the business all day, no one is working on it.

When did you last spend a full day on strategy? An operations director frees that day by running the week, so the growth plan has an owner and a date.

A key person leaving would stop things

If your manager or head of housekeeping left tomorrow, their knowledge would go with them, because it was never written down. That risk grows with every retreat you add.

An operations director builds depth into the team: documented roles, a backup for each task and a handover that does not depend on one person.

What a fractional operations director does

A fractional operations director works with you for a set number of days a month, at a fraction of a full-time salary. In the retreats I work with, the first month goes on the weekly rhythm, the standards that cause the most questions and a single view of the numbers. After that, the focus moves to forecasting, team development and the growth plan you have been putting off.
