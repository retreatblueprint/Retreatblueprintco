8 weeks to high season: a calmer season and guests who come back

Most of your season is already booked. How it goes isn't decided yet.

By Shila Kandiah · October 27, 2026 · 8 min read

In a few weeks your first high-season guests arrive. Most of the season is already booked, but how it goes isn't decided yet. In my experience, venues rarely fix anything once guests are on site. Pickups get missed, meal counts get guessed, and turnovers turn into a scramble that lasts until April. The venues that have a good season use these eight weeks to get every guest, every retreat and every team member ready before the first arrival. That is what decides whether guests leave happy, whether retreat leaders book again, and whether you get through the season with energy left.

Your peak weeks are mostly booked. Your season isn't decided yet.

High season is not one date across Central America. It depends on the country, and in Guatemala there are two.

CountryHigh seasonOther peaks
Costa RicaMid-December to April1Christmas and New Year, when prices peak; Easter week
NicaraguaNovember to April2Not named in the source
PanamaNovember to April2February to mid-April is peak cruise season at Canal ports
GuatemalaNovember to March3A second high season, roughly June to August

Costa Rica guides advise booking 4 to 6 months ahead for popular dates, so most of your peak weeks are probably already booked.1 What is still in your hands is how each stay runs, how smoothly your team works, whether your cash holds through the quiet months, and whether guests and retreat leaders come back.

Eight weeks, one job each

Start the week of Oct 26 and give each week one job. The eight weeks end on Dec 20, five days before Christmas, so nothing new is being built while guests are arriving.

Week 1: See the whole season in one place

Put every retreat of the season on one view, either in your operating system or on a season board on the wall. For each retreat, write down:

  • Retreat leader name and contact
  • Dates, arrivals and departures
  • Group size
  • Transport needed
  • Activities booked or promised

Then mark the overlap days, when one group leaves and another arrives on the same day. Those are your hardest days of the season, and you want to see them now, not the morning of.

Week 2: Check every retreat's logistics and collect what's missing

Go through the season board retreat by retreat and make sure each one is actually set up.

  • Pre-arrival forms. Send them to every booked guest: flights, dietary needs and allergies, emergency contact and preferences. Collect it once, in one place, instead of chasing it on WhatsApp.
  • Itineraries. Ask each retreat leader for their itinerary now, while they still have time to send it.
  • Pickups and drop-offs. Who drives, which vehicle, which flight, and who is the backup driver.
  • Meals. Headcount per day, dietary needs, and when the kitchen needs to know.
  • Activities. Confirm every outside booking with the vendor: tours, surf, massage, excursions.
  • Rooms. A rooming list for every retreat.

Week 3: Get paid and finalize every booking

List every booking with its balance and due date, and send reminders now, not in arrival week. Then finalize the details with each retreat leader: final headcount, rooming list, what is included, and the cancellation policy. Anything unclear now becomes a hard conversation in the middle of a retreat.

Week 4: Plan the cash for the whole season

Work out what each retreat costs you to run: food, transport, extra staff and vendors. Prepay or confirm the suppliers who need it, so peak spending does not drain your account mid-season. Then lay out a month-by-month view until the quiet months end, and decide how much of peak income goes into a reserve before it starts to feel like profit.

Week 5: Get your high-season support in place

Work out when you need extra hands, in housekeeping, the kitchen, driving and guest care, and book them while people are still available. Train them before the first arrival, not during it. Hand off the admin that needs no judgment from you, like confirmations and payment reminders. Then write down who can decide what on their own: a room change, a refund, a late check-in, a complaint. If every question waits for you, you become the bottleneck at the busiest time of the year.

Week 6: Lock every retreat's run sheet

Turn each itinerary into a one-page run sheet: day by day, who does what, and when. Share it with the retreat leader so you are both working from the same plan. Then walk through one guest's stay from arrival to departure: the first hour, each meal, each activity, the last morning. Mark every point where someone could be left waiting, and fix it now.

Week 7: Set up your department messages and team rhythm

Every department needs its own details before each retreat starts. Set up the message each team receives, so it goes out on time for every retreat:

  • Kitchen: names, headcount per meal, dietary needs and allergies, meal times
  • Transportation: names, flights, pickup and drop-off times, the backup driver
  • Activities: names, dates, times, numbers and anything a guide needs to know
  • Housekeeping: arrivals, departures, room changes and turnover times

Then set the team rhythm for the season: a briefing before every retreat, a short check-in each day during it, a turnover plan between groups, and a debrief after.

How I like to do it

I give each department its own portal and share it with them ahead of time, so the kitchen, transport and activities teams can see the upcoming retreats and the details they need whenever they want.

When a venue isn't ready to go digital yet, or a retreat needs it, I also send a clear message to each team a week or a few days before, depending on what is already running. It names the guests, the dates, the to-dos and anything else they need to know. If each department has its own hub spot, I put up a whiteboard there with the details for the retreat that is on.

Week 8: Plan the return visits, then settle in

Guests who come back start before they arrive. Plan to ask each retreat leader about next year's dates while they are still on site, when the experience is fresh. Set up a thank-you and a review request after every stay, and keep a guest list you can contact again, with their permission.

Then settle in. Nothing new launches after Dec 14, so your team works from systems they already know, and anything unfinished waits until January. Brief your team on the first week of guests, and name who covers if you are away or sick.

Your 8-week checklist

  • Week 1 (Oct 26): Every retreat on one season board, with overlap days marked
  • Week 2 (Nov 2): Pre-arrival forms sent, itineraries in, transport, meals, activities and rooms confirmed
  • Week 3 (Nov 9): Final balances requested and booking details finalized with each retreat leader
  • Week 4 (Nov 16): Cost per retreat worked out, suppliers confirmed, season cash view and reserve set
  • Week 5 (Nov 23): Extra staff booked and trained, admin handed off, decision rules written
  • Week 6 (Nov 30): A run sheet for every retreat, shared with the retreat leader
  • Week 7 (Dec 7): Department messages set up and the team rhythm agreed
  • Week 8 (Dec 14): Return-visit plan in place, nothing new after Dec 14, cover plan named
