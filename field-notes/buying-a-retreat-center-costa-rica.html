Buying a Retreat Center in Costa Rica: 9 Checks Before You Sign

The land and buildings get checked. Whether the business can run the day after you get the keys often doesn't.

By Shila Kandiah · October 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Most people buying a retreat center in Costa Rica check the land and the buildings carefully. Far fewer check whether the business can actually run, legally and profitably, the day after they get the keys. These nine checks cover both.

The short answer

  1. Confirm whether the land is titled or a beach concession; the rules for foreigners differ.
  2. Get the land use, water and operating permits in writing, in the current owner's name.
  3. Plan for low season: in 2025, September brought a third as many visitors as December.
  4. Ask for 24 months of booking records, not an occupancy figure.
  5. Find out what the staff come with, and whether the business runs without the owner.

1. Know whether you're buying land or a beach concession

Inland, foreigners can own titled property on the same terms as Costa Ricans. The coast is different. Under the Maritime Zone Law (Law 6043), the first 200 meters from the high-tide line is regulated: the first 50 meters are public, and the next 150 meters are concession land run by the municipality.4

A concession is a long-term right to use public land, not ownership. A foreigner without at least five years of legal residency generally can't hold a majority of one, so ownership is capped at 49%.4 If a beachfront retreat is priced like titled land, ask your lawyer which it is before anything else.

2. Check the land is allowed to be a retreat

The uso de suelo is a municipal certificate that says how a piece of land may legally be used.6 Ask for a current one and check it covers lodging and group events, not only a private home. Many retreats grow room by room; compare what's built today with what the permits show.

3. Follow the water

Guests use far more water than a family home. Construction permits need a water availability letter from the local ASADA or from AyA, the national water utility, and a drilled well usually needs its own concession from the Dirección de Agua.5 Ask for the letter or concession in writing, and ask the staff what happens to water at the end of the dry season, when it's needed most.

If the water runs on an electric pump, a power cut or surge can stop the water too.

From the field

I've often seen a retreat center lose its water while guests were on site, because power surges knocked out the supply. It never shows up in the paperwork, and it's exactly the kind of problem guests remember. Before you buy, ask how often the water has cut out in the past year, and what the backup is: a storage tank big enough for a full house, a backup pump, and a generator to run it. Ask the staff as well as the owner. They're the ones who deal with it.

4. Make sure the business can legally operate

A working lodge in Costa Rica usually touches several offices: a municipal business license (patente), a sanitary operating permit from the Ministry of Health, tax registration and electronic invoicing with Hacienda, and employer registration with the CCSS, the social security fund.7

Ask for each one, check it's current, and check whose name it's in. Two traps:

  • A buyer who closes without municipal tax certifications inherits any unpaid municipal taxes.5
  • If you buy the company that owns the property instead of the property itself, you take on the company's whole history, including any debts or lawsuits.5

5. Ask whether it has an ICT tourism declaration

The Declaratoria Turística is a free, voluntary recognition from the Costa Rica Tourism Institute (ICT) for businesses that meet its legal, technical and economic requirements. As of August 2026, 2,088 businesses held one, 702 of them lodging.3

It isn't required, but it's a useful signal. To qualify, a lodge must be up to date with the CCSS, FODESAF, corporate tax and Hacienda.2 A venue that has one has already had its paperwork checked once. A venue that doesn't is worth a closer look at why.

6. Plan for low season, because it's half the year

Costa Rica's tourism is strongly seasonal, and a retreat's cash flow follows it. In 2025, September brought 104,246 international visitors by air, a third of December's 316,226.1

The drop depends on where the venue is. At Liberia airport, the gateway to Guanacaste, September arrivals were 19% of December's; at San José airport they were 41%.1 A beach venue in the northwest feels low season far harder than one in the central valley.

Ask the seller for monthly revenue and costs, not an annual total. Then check that the business can pay its staff, utilities and loan through September and October from what it earned in high season. Ask what the current owner does in those months: closes, discounts, hosts local groups, or uses the time for repairs.

One of the best uses of those months: list the open dates on short-term rental sites like Airbnb, Vrbo and Booking.com, and offer discounts to fill them. Ask the seller whether they already do, and what those bookings brought in.

7. Ask for booking records, not an occupancy number

"We run at 70% occupancy" tells you nothing until you see the records behind it. Ask for:

  • 24 months of bookings exported from the booking system, by month, with nightly rates
  • the electronic invoices issued through Hacienda for the same period, so revenue can be matched against bookings
  • which bookings came from repeat retreat leaders or groups, and whether those leaders plan to come back under a new owner
  • reviews over the same 24 months, to see whether guest experience is improving or slipping

If a few retreat leaders bring most of the revenue, talk to them before you buy. Their loyalty is often to the current owner, not the venue.

8. Find out what the staff come with

If the business keeps running under you, Costa Rican labor law can treat it as an employer substitution. The previous employer stays jointly liable for up to six months, after which the obligations sit with the new employer alone.8 That includes accrued vacation, severance and the aguinaldo, the year-end bonus paid by December 20.9

Ask for every contract, the payroll, and the CCSS records. Check that everyone who works there is actually on them, and settle with your lawyer who pays for what before closing.

9. Check whether it runs without the owner

This is the check most buyers skip. Many small retreat venues run on the owner's memory: the supplier contacts, the guest emails, the way the kitchen is ordered for a 20-person retreat. When the owner leaves, that knowledge leaves too.

Ask to spend a day watching a normal operation. Then ask:

  • Who handles an inquiry from first email to check-in, and where is that written down?
  • Which tasks only the owner does?
  • Where do the guest list, supplier list and price history live?
  • Will the owner stay for a handover period, and is that in the contract?

Two more that tell you a lot: if the owner were away for two weeks in high season, what would break first? And who holds the logins for the booking system, website, payments and email?

Budget for closing costs

Plan for roughly 3–4% of the price on top, and confirm the exact figure with your notary.

Transfer tax1.5%On the higher of the sale price or the registered fiscal value5
Registry and documentary stampsAbout 0.5–0.8%Paid when the deed is recorded10
Notary and legal feesOften 1–2%Set by a regulated fee schedule, plus 13% VAT on the service11
Capital gains withholding2.5% of the priceOnly if the seller isn't domiciled in Costa Rica; the buyer withholds it12

Buyer and seller often split these, but that is custom, not law, so it's negotiable.5 Budget separately for an operations review, an inspection and the first three months of running costs.

The 9 checks, on one page

  • Titled land or maritime concession, confirmed by your lawyer
  • Current uso de suelo that covers lodging and events
  • Water availability letter or well concession, in writing
  • Patente, health permit, Hacienda and CCSS registrations, current and in the seller's name
  • ICT tourism declaration, or a clear reason there isn't one
  • A low-season budget built from at least 24 months of real numbers
  • Booking records and electronic invoices that match each other
  • Staff contracts, payroll and CCSS records, and an agreement on who pays what
  • A day spent watching operations, and a written handover plan
